Use 1 · Calibrate

Replace my assumptions about "normal" with what real auto loans actually do each month.

Today (synthetic)

  • How often a loan goes 30 days late: my assumption
  • How often a late loan catches up again: my assumption
  • Credit scores, payment sizes, payoffs: my assumption
  • So a model-risk reviewer's first question, "why this threshold?", has a weak answer

With ABS-EE

  • Month-by-month payment history for every loan in public auto pools (for example CarMax Auto Owner Trusts, which file monthly)
  • Measured: late rates, catch-up rates, charge-offs, by credit-score band and loan age
  • The synthetic "normal" borrowers are rebuilt from those measurements
  • Every loan-tape threshold traces to a public dataset and a date
Decision it improves: fewer honest borrowers sent to "worth a look" (today about 30 of every 100 in the proof run), because "unusual" is judged against real behaviour.

The frauds stay planted. Calibration makes the honest background real; it does not create real frauds. The re-tuned system gets scored once on fresh test books.

Use 2 · Benchmark the tape · strengthens S2

A loan tape that performs better than every comparable public pool is a question, not a compliment.

The SEC alleges Tricolor made non-paying or defaulted loans look current. A tape like that shows unusually few late loans. Public pools give the range that honest lenders with a similar credit mix actually report.

Use 4 · Same loans, two reports · a hint for S1 and S2

A lender with public securitizations reports its loans to investors every month. The bank can compare that with the tape it's given.

Bank's loan tape
+
ABS-EE file for the lender's public pools
→
Match loans by fingerprint: origination date, amount, rate, term, make, model, year, state, credit score
→
Same loan in both? Reported differently?

What it can catch

  • A loan pledged to the bank that also sits in a public pool (possible double pledge)
  • A loan current on the bank's tape but charged off in the investor report

Why it's only a hint

  • ABS-EE has no VIN and no borrower ID, by design
  • A fingerprint match is likely, not certain
  • So it opens a question, and the officer asks for the title or VIN to settle it

Tricolor itself is the counter-example: its deals were private (Rule 144A), so there's no ABS-EE to compare against. Per the bank defendants' court filings, reported by Octus (secondary).

Limits, said plainly

SEC data makes the system more real. It doesn't make it see everything.

What it can't do

  • Private deals are invisible. Only registered securitizations file ABS-EE. Tricolor's were private, and many smaller lenders use private deals.
  • No exact loan match. No VIN and no borrower ID, so matching loans is probabilistic.
  • Private borrowers file nothing. The cross-check covers only filers.
  • Public pools come from larger lenders. A small subprime lender is compared within credit bands, and the case says so.

What stays the same

  • Frauds in the test are still planted, so the catch rate is still a design proof, not a real-world rate
  • Rules still decide; SEC data sets the thresholds and adds clues
  • A person still approves every step
  • Any new result is scored once on fresh test books, and every quoted number is updated everywhere
Build plan · what happens after your yes

Seven steps, all on public data, nothing sent anywhere else.

1
Pull ABS-EE files for several public auto-loan trusts, 24 months each, from EDGAR's index. At most 10 requests a second, per SEC rules. Raw files stay local.
2
Measure normal: late, catch-up and charge-off rates by credit band and loan age. The small summary tables get committed.
3
Rebuild honest borrowers in the generator from those measurements.
4
New S2 clue: a tape that performs outside the public peer range.
5
S7 peer benchmarks from XBRL for public non-bank lenders.
6
8-K watcher for Items 2.03 and 2.04, feeding S5.
7
Re-score once on fresh test books, then update the site, deck, resume and case study together.
