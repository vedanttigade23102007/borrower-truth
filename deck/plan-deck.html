In one minute

A real problem from public filings, a fix designed the way big firms do it, and proof you can measure, including where it fails.

The problem

Banks have lent $1.32T to non-bank lenders (FDIC). In the worked example, Wells Fargo lends $208.8B to finance companies and took a fraud loss in this book in Q1 2026 (10-Q). In 2025–26 several such borrowers faked collateral, hid debt and rigged their numbers (SEC and DOJ allegations).

Why one check fails

Each trick hides in a different place: the loan file, the bank account, the invoice, the public lien register, the company registry, the accounts. A check that reads one place is blind to the rest.

What we build

Seven systems, one per trick, passing findings to each other. The output isn't a score but a solved case: what's wrong, the proof, the suggested fix and the drafted paperwork, for one-click human approval. A second pair of eyes, not a fix for fraud.

How the frauds worked · SEC and DOJ documents (allegations)

Two recent cases used seven different tricks between them, as alleged.

Tricolor (car lender, bankrupt Sep 2025)

  • Loans double-pledged to securitizations and warehouse lines; $2.2B pledged against $1.4B real
  • Delinquent "dead loans" reported as current
  • Servicing reports with altered delinquency fields, falsified VINs and adjusted payment terms

A hole of about $800M; lenders owed >$900M. DOJ · SEC complaint, SDNY 26-cv-7041

First Brands (auto parts, bankrupt Sep 2025)

  • Fake and inflated invoices sold to invoice buyers
  • Collateral pledged 2–3 times
  • Hidden debt in related "James Entities"
  • Loan money disguised as customer payments; rigged accounts

~$5B sales, $12M cash, >$9B liabilities. DOJ

Tricolor's CEO, on a recorded call, about when the fraud would fail: "if they sent an auditor and they said, pull this up on your screen" — DOJ SDNY primary

Simple approach (dropped)

1 problem → 1 check → "caught 100%". Toy arithmetic. A real fraudster just uses a different trick.

Big-firm approach (ours)

Map every cause → one system per cause → systems feed each other → one combined decision. Like stacked filters: each has holes, but different holes.
What already exists · checked, not assumed

One tool already guards the collateral pool, backed by Citi and Wells Fargo. No tool we found joins what happens around it for one borrower.

Setpoint PledgeCheck (Oct 2025), "backed by Citi and Wells Fargo"

  • Uniqueness: each asset in only one facility
  • Data integrity: VIN and address consistent
  • Completeness: collections match reports
  • Single record: matches the master file

Works from tapes the borrower uploads. Setpoint release

Outside its view, and where First Brands' tricks sat

  • Hidden debt in related shell companies
  • Fake invoices sold to invoice buyers
  • Loan money disguised as customer payments. A "collections match" passes it, because the cash really arrives
  • Rigged financial statements

Correction made during verification: an earlier draft said PledgeCheck "can't tell if tapes lie". Its own release shows it checks some of that, so we use its result as an input and don't compete with it. In the demo, PledgeCheck results are simulated.

Why the human stays

Model-risk guidance changed on 17 Apr 2026: SR 26-2 / OCC Bulletin 2026-13 replaced SR 11-7 / OCC 2011-12 primary. Here written rules decide. Generative and agentic AI are outside that guidance's scope, so the AI agent only drafts memos and never decides.

A second pair of eyes

Next to covenants and field exams, not instead of them, and not a fix for fraud. The machine does the legwork; an officer decides, under their own name.

Days sooner than a quarterly sample audit

6. Model proof

Why this could fail · said out loud

The design is strong. Its limits are known and stated up front.

Risks

  • Four of the seven systems need private data; the demo uses synthetic data
  • Planted frauds prove coverage, not real-world catch rates
  • A borrower that controls all of its own records can pass (blind red team, round 3)
  • PledgeCheck is simulated; no VIN validity check yet
  • Bulk lien data costs money; the demo uses samples

Rules we keep

  • Public data only; never touch any bank's systems
  • "Open problem", never "loophole"
  • Fraud facts quoted as allegations from SEC/DOJ
  • Never suggest who Wells Fargo's Q1 loss borrower was
  • Fake data labelled; you send every message yourself

What the real data changed

One check tightened (in one real pool, 12% of 60+ day loans turned current without paying). One check redesigned (prime and subprime score curves differ). One new check: in every real pool at least 13.9% of 30-day-late loans are 60+ late a month later; hidden bad loans make late loans vanish.

A design the real data rejected

My first peer benchmark, overall late share against lenders with the same credit mix, would have flagged 3 of 28 honest real pools. It was dropped before it could reach a credit officer.

SEC Form ABS-EE (Reg AB II loan-level data, no VIN, no borrower identity) and data.sec.gov. Tricolor's deals were reportedly private (Rule 144A) and never filed ABS-EE: public data sets the yardstick, it doesn't see every lender. Full tables: proof/REAL_DATA.md.

Four uses of SEC data · each is now a check

Public filings give the bank a second source of truth, and a real yardstick for every decision.

UseWhat it checksWho decides better
1 · CalibrateLoan-tape thresholds set from 2.23M real loan records, not my assumptionsModel risk: each loan-tape threshold traced to public data and a date

Three harder fraud types were added to the test books, each caught only through one of these uses. On the latest fresh run, switching all SEC checks off loses 39 of the 248 caught frauds.

Others have shown the method works on real loans · from the published abstracts, read 14 Oct 2026

Comparing what a borrower reports with independent records finds misreporting, and the misreported loans go bad more often.

Piskorski, Seru and Witkin

Journal of Finance, 2015. Mortgage details disclosed to investors against credit-bureau records: "about one out of every ten loans" misrepresented; delinquencies "more than 60% higher". NBER w18843

Griffin and Maturana

Review of Financial Studies, 2016: "around 48% of loans exhibited at least one indicator of misrepresentation", "associated with a 51% higher likelihood of delinquency".

Griffin and Priest

Journal of Finance, 2023: underwritten income on commercial mortgage-backed loans "is commonly overstated relative to actual property income".

These are mortgage studies, not loans to non-bank lenders: they support the method, not Borrower Truth's numbers.

Around the decision · what a bank's model-risk team asks for

Every decision can be replayed, an open-model agent does the legwork, and anyone can check the answer key.

Decision record

Each verdict carries fingerprints of its inputs, rules and code. Replaying the demo run gave 120 of 120 identical decisions; change one input and the replay names it.

Investigator agent

After the rules decide, an open-weights model (local by default: nothing leaves the bank) checks the registry, liens, courts, payments and GLEIF with read-only tools and drafts a memo. Code drops any line that cites nothing, states a number its source doesn't contain, or contradicts the records on disclosures and liens. It never decides. The demo's memos date from 16 Sep 2026 (Qwen3-8B); stale ones are hidden.

Test it yourself

Honest read of the market

They go deeper in their one place. We connect the places, so we plug into them instead of fighting them.

Where they beat us

  • Setpoint sees other lenders' facilities. We can't. Over 250 lenders and originators use its platform.
  • Quantexa matches "billions of data points". We match names and addresses.
  • Ocrolus spots edited PDFs and AI-made documents. We only read the invoice total.
  • MonetaGo is a registry shared by banks over SWIFT.
  • All of them have real customers and real data. We have synthetic data.

Where we fit: the layer on top

  • PledgeCheck result → S1 already wired in
  • Lien data (e.g. Wolters Kluwer) → S5
  • Entity matching (e.g. Quantexa) → S6
  • Document forensics (e.g. Ocrolus-style) → S4
  • The combiner joins them for one borrower → verdict, proof, fix, approval

Why joining matters: in the alleged First Brands and Tricolor schemes each trick sat in a different place, so a check that watches one place can pass each trick on its own.

The pitch: "More value from tools a bank already backs, by connecting what each one sees about a single borrower. Let me test it on your data, in a lookback pilot inside your network."

Not checked yet: private or enterprise features, pricing, and Moody's, S&P, dv01, Sayari.

How this would actually get approved at a bank

A tool isn't adopted because it works. A sponsor carries it, control partners sign it off, and a committee approves a small, safe first step.

1 · Socialize
Ask former credit officers and field examiners for advice first, find the roadblocks, then an executive sponsor who owns NDFI credit risk.
2 · Draft
One-page memo for the sponsor; a business case (problem, requirements, resources, timeline, money, alternatives).
3 · Control partners
Model risk, BSA/AML compliance, legal, InfoSec, third-party risk, operational risk, audit.
4 · Committee
Present with the sponsor, defend the metrics, alternatives and failure scenarios. Ask for a 90-day shadow pilot, not a rollout.

Executive sponsor

Head of the NDFI lending business, or its senior credit officer. They own the loss, so they own the case.

Business owner

The credit officers and portfolio managers who would work the queue. Their verdict is the pilot's success measure.
